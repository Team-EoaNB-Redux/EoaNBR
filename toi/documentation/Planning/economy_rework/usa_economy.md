# United States c.1856–57 — Industry Data

Oct 31, 2026 · @Someone

In mid-1857, on the eve of the Panic, the United States had about 29 million people, nearly 4 million of them enslaved. National income was roughly $4bn (\~£820m), more per head than the UK. The economy was the world's largest cotton grower, a rising grain, meat and lumber producer, and a manufacturing power concentrated in New England and the Middle States. Foreign trade in the year to 30 June 1857 was about $361m each way including gold, or $279m of domestic exports.

## How to read this

- Basis tags as before: **S** = checked against a linked source this session; **H** = standard historical figure (US censuses, *Historical Statistics of the US*, Swank, Treasury reports), recalled; **E** = my estimate.
- **Why FY1857:** the federal trade year ran 1 July 1856 – 30 June 1857. It closes almost two months before the Panic broke on 24 August 1857, so FY1857 is a clean pre-Panic baseline. Crops are the 1856 harvest. The 1860 census (covering crops of 1859) is the best detailed benchmark; I note where it differs from 1856–57.
- Currency: $4.86 = £1 (gold par). Tons are short tons (2,000 lb) unless marked.

### Scale of the economy

- GNP was about $4.1bn in 1859 (Gallman, H), so about $4.0bn in 1857 (£820m). That is roughly $140 or £29 a head, against roughly £21 in the UK and £2 in India (E).
- Labour force in 1860 was about 11.1m including enslaved workers (Lebergott, H): agriculture \~53%, manufacturing \~14%, trade \~8%, construction \~5%, domestic service \~5%, transport \~2%, mining \~1.5%.
- Manufacturing in 1860: 140,433 establishments, 1.31m employees, $1.89bn gross product (H).

## Resource industries

In 1856–57 the US mined roughly 14m tons of coal (UK: 65m long tons). It smelted about 880,000 tons of pig iron (UK: 3.66m). It was the world's biggest gold producer after Australia, at about $55m a year from California. It was also a huge lumber producer and a minor producer of lead and copper. Pennsylvania dominated coal and iron.

## Agricultural industries

Farming employed about 5.6m people in 1857 (E), roughly half the workforce, including about 2.5m enslaved field workers. Gross farm output was roughly $1.3–1.5bn (E). Corn and hogs were the largest by volume, but cotton was the money crop. Cotton earned $131.6m of exports in FY1857 (H), about 47% of all domestic exports. Southern staples (cotton, tobacco, rice, naval stores) made up $158m of the $279m of domestic exports (S).

## Manufacturing

US manufacturing produced about $1.7–1.8bn gross a year by 1856–57 (E, between the census values of $1.02bn in 1850 and $1.89bn in 1860, H). It employed about 1.2m people. Two-thirds of it sat in New England and the Middle States. By value added, cotton textiles was the leading industry in 1860; by gross output, flour milling. Iron, machinery and woollens were the other modern industries. Boots and shoes, clothing, lumber and leather were large but small-shop trades (S, NBER).

### Textile and manufacturing geography for the game

- **New England:** textiles (cotton and wool), shoes, machinery, firearms, clocks, paper. Water-powered mill towns use mainly young women and, increasingly, Irish immigrant labour.
- **Middle States:** New York has clothing, printing, sugar refining and finance. Philadelphia has the most diversified industry: locomotives, chemicals, carpets, textiles and iron. Pittsburgh has iron, glass and steam engines. New Jersey has silk (Paterson), leather and machinery.
- **Old Northwest:** flour, meatpacking, whisky, farm implements, lumber, steamboats. Cincinnati is the West's largest manufacturing city; Chicago is rising fast.
- **South:** about 8% of manufacturing. Tobacco processing (Richmond, Petersburg), the Tredegar Iron Works (Richmond), turpentine distilling, flour, a few cotton mills, sugar mills, and cotton gins (uncounted, and alone worth roughly $200m in ginned value).

## Intangibles

Services employed about 2.5m Americans by 1857 (E) and produced perhaps 30–35% of national income. The defining services were transport and finance. The US had about 24,500 miles of railroad in 1857, nearly as much as the rest of the world combined (H). Its merchant fleet, about 5.4m tons, was close to Britain's. And it had about 1,400 note-issuing state banks with no central bank. Entertainment was commercial and popular rather than court-sponsored.

## Trade totals, FY1857 (year to 30 June 1857)

In the last full pre-Panic year the US imported $360.9m and exported $363.0m, both counts including gold and silver (S). Merchandise alone ran a deficit of about $55m (imports \~$348m against \~$294m of domestic and re-exported goods). It was paid for with about $69m of California gold shipped out. The US exported raw materials (cotton above all) and food, and imported manufactures, sugar and coffee.

## Panic of 1857: timeline and impacts

The Panic was a short, sharp financial crash: August–December 1857, with depression lingering into 1859. It hit Northern railroads, banks, grain farmers and industrial workers hardest. The cotton South escaped lightly, which fed the Southern belief that "Cotton is King" (S). Contemporaries reported failures in Liverpool, London, Paris, Hamburg, Oslo and Stockholm, making it the first truly global crisis (S).

### Causes already in place at the mod's start

- Railroad and Western land speculation; railroad stocks peaked in July 1857 (S).
- The Crimean War grain boom (1854–56) ended as Russian wheat returned, so Western wheat prices and farm mortgages were overextended.
- The Tariff of March 1857 cut duties (from about 26% to under 20% on dutiable goods over 1856–60, S), cutting federal revenue just before the slump.
- The Dred Scott decision (March 1857) unsettled Kansas and Nebraska land and railroad values (S).
- Heavy reliance on British credit and on gold shipments from California.

### Political after-effects (1857–61)

Northern manufacturers and Pennsylvania ironmasters pushed for protection, which fed the Republican platform and the Morrill Tariff of 1861. Southern leaders concluded the cotton economy was crisis-proof. The federal government ran deficits and borrowed for the first time since the 1840s. All of this sharpened the sectional divide before 1860.
