# British Isles Economy c.1857 — Industry Data

Oct 31, 2026 · @Someone

1857 is a good anchor year. Hunt's *Mineral Statistics* (from 1854) and the Board of Trade's computed import values (from 1854) both cover it, and the 1851 and 1861 censuses bracket it for employment. The weak spots are country and regional splits, which the trade accounts never made: Ireland's trade with Britain was domestic after 1826. Agriculture is also weak, because Britain had no farm census until 1866, although Ireland did.

## How to read this

- Every table row carries a basis tag. **S** = verified against a linked page this session. **H** = standard historical figure (Hunt, Mitchell, Deane & Cole), recalled but not re-opened here. **E** = my estimate or interpolation. Treat H as good and E as a sensible placeholder for balancing.
- Units: £1 = 20 shillings (s) = 240 pence (d). Ton = long ton (2,240 lb). Quarter (qr) = 8 bushels, about 480–500 lb of wheat.
- Employment figures are mostly the 1851 Great Britain census, which excludes Ireland. Some 1861 comparison figures from the same compiled table appear to be England & Wales only; for example linen falls from 98,860 to 22,050 when Dundee drops out. Use the 1851 values as the GB baseline.

## Resource industries

In 1857 the UK raised about 65.4 million tons of coal and about 3.6 million tons of pig iron. Coal and iron together outweighed every other mineral industry combined by roughly 10 to 1 in value. Copper, tin and lead were already past their peak or close to it, and Britain relied on imported timber.

Copper smelting was concentrated at Swansea and Neath in Wales. Swansea smelted most Cornish ore plus imported Chilean, Cuban and Australian ore. For game purposes, the *mining* belongs to England and Ireland and the *refining* to Wales. A sample 1857 price: Levant mine sold 1,587 t of ore holding 81.6 t of copper for £7,446, about £4 14s per ton of ore.

### Wood and logging

Britain was already a heavy timber importer. About two-thirds of supply came from Canada and the Baltic (Prussia, Sweden, Norway, Russia); imported timber, deals and staves were worth roughly £10m in 1857. Domestic woodland covered roughly 4% of Great Britain and much less of Ireland. It supplied oak bark for tanning, coppice wood, pit props, hop poles and some shipbuilding oak, worth perhaps £1.5–2m a year (E). Workers: sawyers 35,443 (1851), plus about 10,000 woodmen and foresters (E), plus coopers and wheelwrights counted under manufacturing.

## Agricultural industries

Agriculture was still the largest single employer, at roughly 3 million people across the UK. It produced about a fifth of British national income in 1851, and much more in Ireland. Its gross output was roughly £200–230m a year (E). Britain was a large and growing net food importer: in 1857 it imported about £19m of grain and about £25m of colonial "luxuries". Ireland was a net food exporter to Britain.

## Manufacturing

Textiles were the core of British manufacturing in 1857. The UK's five factory textile trades employed 682,497 people in 1856, and cotton alone exported about £39m. Iron and engineering came next. Steel was still a craft product until Bessemer, whose process was announced in 1856 and became commercial only around 1860.

### Chemicals by region

The Leblanc alkali trade was concentrated on the Tyne (South Shields, Newcastle), in Lancashire (St Helens, Widnes, Runcorn) and in Glasgow (Tennant's St Rollox, then the largest chemical works in the world). These produced soda ash, crystals, bleaching powder and sulphuric acid for glass, soap and textile bleaching. In 1856 William Perkin, in London, made mauveine, the first synthetic dye; commercial production began in 1857. Ireland and Wales had little chemical industry, apart from Swansea's copper-smoke by-products and Wicklow pyrites, which were shipped to British acid makers.

## Intangibles

Services employed roughly 2.5–3m people in Great Britain by 1857 and produced roughly 40% of national income (E). Domestic service alone was the second-largest occupation after farm labour. Entertainment was small in money terms, at perhaps 1–1.5% of income (E), but highly visible: the 1857 Manchester Art Treasures Exhibition drew 1.3m visitors.

## Trade totals, 1857

In 1857 the UK imported £187.8m of merchandise and exported £146.2m, of which £122.1m was British and Irish produce. That leaves a visible deficit of £41.7m. Shipping, insurance and overseas investment income covered the gap, so the overall balance was in surplus. 1857 was the peak year of the 1848–57 cycle; the crash of late 1857 cut 1858 exports to £116.6m.

### A note on Ireland

After 1826, trade between Ireland and Great Britain was treated as coastal (domestic) trade, so Ireland has no separate foreign-trade account for 1857. In practice Ireland ran a large surplus with Britain, perhaps £20–25m a year of cattle, pigs, butter, grain, eggs and linen. It imported British coal, textiles, iron goods and colonial goods re-exported through Liverpool and Glasgow (E). For a game model, treat Ireland as an agricultural exporter whose foreign trade flows through British ports.
